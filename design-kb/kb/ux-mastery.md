# UI/UX mastery: principles and foundations

Built 2026-11-12. Ten Haiku agents each researched one area on the live web; the main session read all ten reports and re-checked only the two legal claims that go stale fastest (DOJ Title II, FTC click-to-cancel). Everything else is as the agents reported it.

**Tags.** `[verified]` = an agent saw it in a fetched page (URL in section 15). `[rechecked]` = the main session also confirmed it. `[estimated]` = judgment or common practice, not tested. `[assumed]` = background knowledge, no source fetched. `unknown` = could not be established. Rules are paraphrased. Where a section says "not covered", treat it as a gap, not as "nothing to say".

**Evidence tags.** `[experiment]` = peer-reviewed study. `[meta-analysis]` = pooled peer-reviewed studies. `[industry]` = non-peer-reviewed study or report (NN/g, Baymard, UXmatters, IBM report). `[standard]` = W3C, ISO or platform spec. `[convention]` = practitioner rule with no research base. Verified bibliography, DOIs and confidence levels: `kb/references.md` (source of truth where it disagrees with this file).

**Evidence gaps.** No peer-reviewed evidence was found for APCA versus WCAG 2 contrast, vestibular-safe UI animation (ground it in WCAG 2.3.3 and `prefers-reduced-motion`), design-system effectiveness, or foldable and responsive layout (use platform docs). Treat these as `[convention]`.

## 1. What mastery is (a working model) [estimated]

Seniority is mostly scope and judgment, not polish; the only sources found were practitioner blogs, so this is opinion. A usable model has five layers, each depending on the one below:

1. **Perception and cognition**: how people see, attend, remember, decide (section 2).
2. **Principles**: heuristics and conceptual models that turn that into review criteria (3).
3. **Craft**: hierarchy, layout, type, color, states, motion, accessibility (4 to 7).
4. **Structure and evidence**: information architecture, writing, research, systems (8 to 10).
5. **Judgment**: framing the problem, tying design to outcomes, ethics, saying "it depends, and here is on what" (11).

The usual failure is skipping layer 1 and 2 and collecting layer 3 techniques. The second most common is memorising the "laws" without knowing which are contested (section 2 table).

## 2. Perception and cognition

Mechanism first: the visual system groups before it reads, attention is scarce, working memory is small, and people reuse expectations from elsewhere.

| Idea | Defensible claim | Known limits |
|---|---|---|
| Gestalt (proximity, similarity, closure, continuity, figure-ground) | Spatial and visual proximity groups elements; group controls by spacing before borders [assumed] | Effect sizes unknown; no checked review retrieved |
| F-pattern | Default scanning of unformatted text: top sweep, shorter second sweep, left vertical scan; NN/g's original study had 232 users and an NN/g follow-up (participant count not confirmed); heatmaps, no formal statistics [industry, not peer-reviewed] [verified] | A default, not a law (NN/g: a "dominant reading pattern", a "rough, general shape"; E and inverted-L variants exist); headings and formatting override it; weaker in nav menus |
| Pre-attentive features | Hue, size, orientation, motion can be found in roughly 200 to 250 ms; combinations of features usually need serial search [verified] | Many highlights cancel each other |
| Cognitive load | Intrinsic, extraneous, germane: the 1998 model treats them as additive; a 2019 model separates germane processing; a 2021 review argues extraneous load is not always harmful [verified] | Removing all friction can remove useful effort |
| Recognition over recall | Visible options cue memory [verified] | NN/g cites no controlled experiment; rests on theory |
| Jakob's law | Users carry conventions from other products; a new pattern can win if the gain outweighs the learning cost [verified] | Voice, AR and AI patterns have no settled conventions |
| Miller's law | Miller (1956) said about 7; Cowan (2001) concluded about 4 chunks on average (population about 3 to 5, individuals about 2 to 6) [verified]. Miller's paper covers two separate limits (absolute judgment about 4 to 8 alternatives; span about 7, varying by material: digits, letters, words) | **Contested.** Do not cap menus at 7; use about 4 for working memory, not for menu length |
| Hick's law | Choice reaction time grows with log of the number of equally likely options; usual form T = a + b log2(n+1) [assumed form] [verified law] | Not a search law; random-list search is roughly linear; familiar verbal responses show little effect. It applies to equally probable alternatives and is modified by practice and stimulus-response compatibility, so fewer menu items is not automatically faster; do not read Hick as "fewer options always convert better" (see choice overload in section 9) |
| Fitts's law | Movement time depends on distance and target width, Shannon form ID = log2(D/W + 1) [assumed constants]; constants are device-specific [verified] | No universal milliseconds; compare only within one device. Surviving rules: screen edges and corners act as very large targets for a mouse; cluster related controls. Touch and 2D targets are not well modelled by the mouse constants |
| Doherty threshold | Doherty and Thadani (1982, IBM report GE20-0752): an IBM report, not a journal article and not peer-reviewed [industry]; it argues productivity rises sharply as response time falls below 1 s. The 400 ms figure is a later popularisation read off the curve, not written in the report [convention] [UNVERIFIED against a primary scan]; it is a productivity claim, not an attention study | Historical claim; do not present 400 ms as an attention law; not validated for all tasks or networks |
| Peak-end | Kahneman et al. (1993) cold-water study: people preferred the longer trial with a milder end [verified] | Tests on real events are mixed: Kemp 2008 (https://doi.org/10.3758/mc.36.1.132) found some support but not a strong predictor; Miron-Shatz 2009 found day-long experiences did not follow it (boundary-condition tests, not replications of the 1993 study); effect size unknown |
| Zeigarnik | A 2025 meta-analysis found no memory advantage for interrupted tasks, but a tendency to resume them [verified] | Do not cite it to justify progress bars or "boost completion" claims |

Practical rule [estimated]: treat these as prompts for a design question ("is the primary target big and near?"), never as a number to quote.

## 3. Heuristics and design fundamentals

- **Nielsen's 10 heuristics** (language lightly refined 2020; titles unchanged since 1994) [verified]: visibility of system status; match with the real world; user control and freedom; consistency and standards; error prevention; recognition over recall; flexibility and efficiency; aesthetic and minimalist design; help users recognise, diagnose and recover from errors; help and documentation.
- **Norman's six**: affordances, signifiers, constraints, mappings, feedback, conceptual model [verified]. An affordance is what an object permits; a signifier is the cue that shows it. Gulfs of execution and evaluation are the gap between goal and available actions, and between system state and what the user perceives [assumed; not found in a fetched source].
- **Shneiderman's 8 golden rules** (1985; 6th ed. 2016; dates unsourced) [verified titles]: consistency; universal usability; informative feedback; dialogs that yield closure; error prevention; easy reversal; user in control; low short-term memory load.
- **Overlap**: feedback, error prevention and memory load appear in all three sets. **Tension** [estimated]: Tesler's law and minimalism push to hide complexity; Shneiderman's "user in control" warns hidden controls can block goals. Tesler: irreducible complexity must sit with the system or the user; simplifying invites harder tasks [verified].
- **Direct manipulation**: act on visible objects with immediate results; strain and accidental activation are risks, and bulk work is faster with shortcuts [verified].
- **Heuristic evaluation limits.** NN/g calls heuristics guidelines, not laws; a violation is not automatically a problem; it cannot replace user research; it recommends 3 to 5 independent evaluators [verified]. Nielsen and Landauer (1993, https://doi.org/10.1145/169059.169166) [experiment]: fitted per-evaluator detection about 0.31; formula 1-(1-0.31)^n gives about 84% at five evaluators [estimated arithmetic]; their cost-benefit optimum was about 4.4 evaluators for medium to large projects [verified].
- **Evaluator effect** [experiment]: Hertzum and Jacobsen (2001, *IJHCI* 13(4), 421-443, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327590ijhc1304_05): across 11 studies, average agreement between two evaluators ranged from 5% to 65%. Treat "3 to 5 evaluators" as a planning heuristic and expect different evaluators to find different problems.
- **Review method** [estimated]: list every error path as prevented, recovered or unhandled; check every flow has an exit; check one term maps to one action.
- Progressive disclosure: shows secondary options on request, risk is undiscovered features [assumed; the NN/g page failed to load].

## 4. Visual design

- **Hierarchy comes from contrast with neighbours**, not from color alone. NN/g's example tiers: 14 to 16 px body, 18 to 22 px subheads, up to 32 px headers, at most about two large elements; these are expert recommendations, not tested [verified]. Squint test: blur the screen and see what survives [verified suggestion].
- **Proximity and CRAP** (contrast, repetition, alignment, proximity) [verified]. Internal padding should not exceed the space between groups, so groups read as distinct [verified]. CRAP is a heuristic, not an experimentally validated set [assumed].
- **Spacing scale.** Why 8 pt: whole pixels render cleanly at 1x to 4x, and 8 divides evenly by 2 and 4. Scale: 4, 8, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, 96; use 4 only for micro-adjustments [verified]. Whether the base is 4 or 8 is convention [assumed].
- **Grid example (web)**: 12 columns, 24 px gutter, 60 px side margins on a 1440 px artboard [verified]. Apple tvOS insets (60 pt top and bottom, 80 pt sides) are platform-specific [verified].
- **Whitespace and density**: whitespace helps grouping, but too much can hurt findability and push content below the fold [verified]; experts tolerate higher density, onboarding favors lower, and letting users choose density is recommended [verified].
- **Elevation**: Material uses six levels (0 to +5), with 0 to 3 resting and +4, +5 for interaction; the dp values were not retrieved [verified]. Refactoring UI: prefer shadows, background contrast or spacing over borders [verified]. Shadow values: [estimated].
- **Radius, imagery**: consistency conventions only; one radius across buttons and cards [assumed].
- **Iconography**: see the subsection after this list.
- **"Template-looking" UI** tells [estimated, agent judgment]: no spacing scale, equal type size and weight, borders everywhere, generic saturated accent. Evidence strength: strong for hierarchy via size and contrast and for the pixel-grid rationale; moderate for NN/g and Apple numeric guidance; weak or taste for ratios, shadows, radius and "professional".
- **Aesthetics and usability, with limits** [experiment]: Tractinsky, Katz and Ikar (2000, *Interacting with Computers* 13(2), https://doi.org/10.1016/s0953-5438(00)00031-x) found perceived aesthetics related to perceived usability; Hassenzahl and Monk (2010, *Human-Computer Interaction* 25(3), https://doi.org/10.1080/07370024.2010.500139) found the inference is weaker after real use. Do not claim that polish makes products usable.

### Hierarchy, spacing and composition: focused pass (added 2026-11-12, four Haiku agents; not re-checked by the main session)

**Hierarchy: the empirical base is small** [verified unless noted]
- Leiva et al. (MobileHCI 2020 preprint): 30 participants free-viewed 193 mobile screenshots for 3.5 s each. Top-left drew 43.3% of fixations vs 13.1% for bottom-right; element area showed no meaningful link to fixations; text drew strong fixation. Limits: Chinese-language UIs, free viewing, not task-based.
- Burmistrov et al. (NordiCHI 2016, preliminary): 24 participants, word search on 12 pt text; Normal and Bold searched faster than Light, and thin or low-contrast gray text raised fixation duration. Size was not varied.
- Djamasbi, Siegel and Tullis (2010), "Generation Y, web design, and eye tracking", IJHCS 68(5), 307-323: Gen-Y preferences and fixations; large images and faces drew early fixations and text drew few (98 raters, 19 eye-tracked). It is not a visual-hierarchy paper; the closer source is Djamasbi, Siegel and Tullis (2011), "Visual hierarchy and viewing behavior", HCI International, 331-340 (seen only via Google Scholar).
- Lindgaard et al. (2006): visual-appeal ratings were consistent at 50 ms exposure (n not seen).
- NN/g F-pattern [industry, not peer-reviewed]: the original study used 232 users; an NN/g follow-up exists (participant count not found on the page that could be opened; the earlier 45 to 47 figure is unsourced). Heatmaps only, no formal statistics; weakens with good visual cues.
- **No controlled study** isolating size, weight, contrast, position or color on task completion was found, and **no evidence-based cap** on emphasis levels. NN/g's "at most 3 sizes, 3 contrast variations, 2 large elements" is labeled guidance, not research.
- Techniques from systems: Material button tiers run filled (high emphasis), filled tonal and elevated, outlined (medium), text (low). GOV.UK: one default button for the main call to action because several weaken each other; warning buttons sparingly and never red alone; heading size classes are kept separate from semantic level. Apple: most important content near the top-leading corner; alignment and indentation show subordination; space or containers group.
- Diagnostics: squint test (blur and check that focus, groups and interactive elements still read); NN/g's blurred Spotify example showed an unintended dominant item. The 5-second test has no peer-reviewed validation found; grayscale test: no source. A critique notes the five-second method rests on one study of visual complexity, not layout [unknown strength].

**Spacing in real systems** [verified]
- Atlassian: 8 px base; 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 40, 48, 64, 80 px as `space.*` tokens.
- Carbon v10: `spacing-01` to `-13` = 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 64, 80, 96, 160 px; closer reads as more related, more space signals importance; deviating from the scale is discouraged. v11 values not loaded.
- GOV.UK: units 1 to 9 = 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50, 60 px on large screens; units 4 to 9 shrink on small screens (switch at 640 px); units 0 to 3 never change; px only.
- Utopia fluid scale: 3xs about 5 px up to 3xl about 108 to 120 px, interpolated with `clamp()` between 360 and 1240 px viewports.
- Reading the differences: Atlassian and Carbon are fixed stepped scales (2 to 4 px at the low end), GOV.UK is a 5 px rhythm that steps by breakpoint, Utopia is a ratio scale. Pick one and reference tokens, not raw values. Material 3 and Polaris values not loaded.
- **Spacing relative to type**: WCAG 1.4.12 overrides are multiples of font size (line height 1.5x, paragraph 2x, letter 0.12x, word 0.16x), so use them as a stress test. For buttons and inputs, equal vertical padding, font size, line height and border keep heights equal; line-height exactly 1 makes inputs taller than buttons, so use about 1.2 or `normal`; prefer padding over fixed height. Em vs rem padding: unknown.
- **Whitespace evidence is small-sample.** [UNSOURCED: no record of this study found; Scharff and Ahumada (2002), https://doi.org/10.1167/2.9.7, concerns text contrast over backgrounds, not line spacing, so do not cite it for spacing] Scharff and Williams (14 participants): line spacing mattered (F(2,22)=8.24, p<.01), single spacing slower than 1.5 or double, authors suggest at least 12 pt (units in the source are inconsistent; publication status unchecked). Chaparro et al. (2004): margins improved reading speed and comprehension; leading did not significantly change performance (no numbers on the page). A Canadian Journal of Learning and Technology study found content framed by whitespace was better understood; line length had no effect. Paragraph-spacing effect sizes: unknown.
- **Density**: Material (Material 2 era web article) steps 0 to -3, each step cuts component height 4 px (a 36 px button becomes 24 px), does not change internal horizontal spacing, keeps touch targets at least 48 px, and margins and gutters should grow as density rises. Cloudscape: comfortable by default, compact for data-heavy views, not applied to alerts, help panels or small targets, applied app-wide.

**Layout grids in real systems** [verified]
- Carbon 2x: 320 px = 4 columns, 672 px = 8 columns, 1056 px = 16 columns, 1312 px and 1584 px = 16 columns (80 and 96 px columns); outer margin 0 at 320 px, 16 px at 672 to 1312 px, 24 px at 1584 px; gutter 32 px (16 px padding each side); gutterless grids for closely related content.
- GOV.UK: 1020 px default max page width; main content in a two-thirds column even without a sidebar; lines usually under about 75 characters; single column first.
- Material window classes (width): compact under 600 dp, medium 600 to 839, expanded 840 to 1200, large 1200 to 1600, extra-large 1600 and up; height compact under 480, medium 480 to 900, expanded 900 and up. M3 margins, gutters and columns: unknown (page did not load).
- Apple: lay out by size class per axis, not by device or orientation; only tvOS numbers found (60 pt top and bottom, 80 pt sides; two-column grid 860 pt wide, 40 pt horizontal and 100 pt minimum vertical spacing). iOS margins and readable width: unknown.
- **Proportion systems**: one 2024 study (100 young Italian-speaking adults) found golden-ratio dot patterns rated more pleasant than random ones (4.0 vs 2.7 on a 7-point scale), but compared them only with random dot patterns (Salera et al. 2024, *Symmetry* 16(3), https://www.mdpi.com/2073-8994/16/3/333), not with other ordered layouts or proportions, and implicit and explicit measures did not correlate. No UI study testing golden ratio, rule of thirds or modular scales against each other was found [estimated from search]. Treat them as taste.
- **Layout patterns**: NN/g's eye-tracking report (over 500 participants, three studies) found scanning is the default, with F, layer-cake, zigzag and lawn-mower patterns by page type; layer-cake fixations cluster on headings (that article's examples use 9 and 1 participants). No quantitative evidence for Z-pattern, card grids, sidebars or bento [unknown]. Baymard (19 e-commerce sites, more than 700 issues, 83 guidelines): abandonment of 67 to 90% for mediocre product-list usability vs 17 to 33% when slightly optimized; list-vs-grid finding not found.
- Optical alignment and keyline numbers: only secondary sources, not fetched [assumed].

### Iconography (added 2026-11-12, main-session search; one NN/g page fetched, the rest are practitioner sources)

**Usability evidence** (NN/g, qualitative; no sample sizes or method reported) [verified]
- Users read icons through past experience. Only a few (home, print, search) are widely recognised; most are ambiguous across products. Heart and star may mean favorite, bookmark, love or rating depending on the site; a clock icon for history went unclicked by every participant in one study.
- Keep labels visible at all times. Hover-only labels add interaction cost and fail on touch; even standard icons benefit from labels, especially modified ones.
- Prominence changes with screen size: one example's menu icon took about 20% of mobile nav width but about 3.5% on desktop. Show navigation outright on large screens rather than collapsing it into an icon.
- Method: research competitor and platform-standard icons first; keep icons simple and schematic; if finding an icon for a concept takes over five seconds, it probably will not communicate; test recognisability (what do people expect it to mean) and memorability (do they still know it after about two weeks).
- Icons do help when familiar and culturally appropriate: compact, easy to tap, no translation, visual consistency across a product family.

**Craft rules** (practitioner convention, not research) [estimated]
- **One stroke weight per set.** Typically 1.5 or 2 px on a 24 px grid, applied to every icon including internal details; one corner radius, one cap style, one style (outline or filled). Do not mix filled and outlined versions of the same icon at the same hierarchy level, since the weight difference reads as emphasis.
- **Stroke by size is contested.** One guide pairs 16 px with a 1 px stroke and 24 px with 1.5 px; another calls 2 px the 24 px convention (Lucide, Heroicons, Feather). Pick one scale and document it.
- **Optical, not mathematical, alignment.** Circles and triangles need different positioning than squares; build keyline shapes (square, circle, rectangles) and snap every icon to them; one example shrinks square icons 1 px inside a 16 px boundary.
- **Pixel sharpness.** For 1 px strokes, offset shapes by 0.5 px so they land on the pixel grid and avoid anti-aliased blur.
- **Match icon weight to adjacent text weight** so the icon reads as part of the text (regular body with a 1.5 to 2 px icon; bold heading with a bold or filled one).
- **Governance**: document grid, stroke, radius and naming; keep guidelines and assets in sync.
- A "pass if at least 80% of testers predict the action, otherwise add a label" rule appears in one framework document with no study behind it; treat it as a team convention at most [assumed].

**Focused-pass additions** (Haiku agent, not re-checked) [verified unless noted]
- **Recognition studies are small.** ISO 9186:2001 uses a 66% recognition benchmark; ISO 3864 treats above 66.7% as good for signs, not computer icons. Ashe, Eardley and Fletcher (2018, ASTESJ 3(6), https://www.astesj.com/v03/i06/p37/) studied a virtual-gallery interface only: 33.3% of 21 icons clearly identified out of context [experiment, low-tier journal]; do not generalise to mobile icons (an earlier draft quoted 38.1% vs 76.2%; use the bibliography figure). Context matters more than the icon alone.
- **Solid vs outline**: a large unmoderated test (1,260 tests, over 1,000 participants, 20 tab-bar icons, mostly young and tech-savvy) found hollow icons about 0.1 s slower on average, with no significant difference for black-on-white icons and variation per icon. A second summary (no sample size) reports solid generally faster. Apple's HIG rule is the strongest published guidance: outline for toolbars and text-adjacent use, fill for tab bars and selection. No study on selected vs unselected states.
- **Icon-only vs labeled**: the only controlled comparison found had 15 people and no significant performance difference (small sample). No large peer-reviewed study. NN/g's hamburger study (179 users, six sites) found hidden navigation costs more on mobile, and calls the hamburger viable but not universal.
- **SF Symbols**: nine weights matched to San Francisco weights; three scales (small, medium default, large) defined relative to cap height; rendering modes monochrome, hierarchical, palette, multicolor. Exact point sizes per scale: unknown.
- **Material Symbols axes**: FILL 0 to 1 (default 0), wght 100 to 700 (default 400), GRAD about -50 to 200 (default 0), opsz 20 to 48 dp. Material 1 metrics: 24 dp icon, 48 dp touch target, toolbar icons on a 4 dp baseline grid. M3 keylines and padding: unknown.
- **Accessibility**: icons needed to understand content need 3:1 against adjacent colors (WCAG 1.4.11), with exceptions for decorative or redundant graphics; targets 24 x 24 CSS px at AA (2.5.8), 44 x 44 at AAA (2.5.5); decorative images use `alt=""`; icon-only controls need an accessible name; `aria-hidden` on decorative inline SVG is common practice [assumed].
- **Cultural variation** (agency-blog anecdote citing "Amazon India" research; no primary source found, treat as anecdote): a magnifier read as a "ping-pong bat"; people with no large-supermarket experience had no model for a trolley, and a bag tested as more universal. Floppy-disk and mailbox icons are called age- or US-specific with no study named.

**Not covered**: ISO 9186 acceptance criteria and sample sizes, exact SF Symbols point sizes, Material 3 icon keylines and padding, selected-state studies, large-sample icon-only vs labeled evidence.

## 5. Typography and color

**Typography**
- **Apple size floors** (min / default): iOS 11 / 17 pt; macOS 10 / 13; tvOS 23 / 29; visionOS 12 / 17; watchOS 12 / 16. Avoid Ultralight, Thin and Light at small sizes; avoid tight leading on three or more lines [verified]. iOS Body is 17 pt on 22 pt leading (about 1.29); macOS Body 13 on 16 [verified].
- **Text spacing (WCAG 1.4.12)**: content must survive user overrides of line height 1.5x, paragraph spacing 2x, letter spacing 0.12x, word spacing 0.16x [verified]. Fixed-pixel line heights break this.
- **Measure (characters per line)**: Bringhurst 45 to 75, 66 ideal; Dyson and Haselgrove (2001) about 55 gave the best screen balance; Dyson and Kipping (1998) found 100 cpl read faster than 25 with equal comprehension [verified, via Wikipedia summary]. Full-width desktop body text often exceeds 100 [verified].
- **Tabular figures**: `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums` works only if the font ships the `tnum` feature [verified].
- Modular ratios (1.125, 1.2, 1.25, 1.333), two families at most, `clamp()` for fluid type [estimated]. MDN: keep max at least twice min so text can reach 200% zoom [verified].

**Color**
- **Perceptual spaces**: Oklab/OKLCH fit perceptual data better than CIELAB on lightness (reported RMS error 0.20 vs 1.70; HSV 11.59); for hue JzAzBz (0.43) beat Oklab (0.49) [verified]. CIELAB drifts toward purple in blues [verified]. CSS Color 4 defines `lch()` and `oklch()` [verified]. Mistakes: assuming equal L looks equally bright across hues; using HSL lightness to set contrast; gamut clipping [assumed].
- **WCAG 2.x contrast**: 4.5:1 normal text, 3:1 large (18 pt, or 14 pt bold), 7:1 AAA, 3:1 for UI components and graphics. Ratio = (L1+0.05)/(L2+0.05) on linearised sRGB [verified].
- **APCA is not a standard.** The 8 April 2026 WCAG 3 draft still lists the contrast algorithm as to be determined; APCA was dropped from the July 2023 draft; completion estimated 2030 at the earliest [verified]. Do not cite Lc values as WCAG conformance. No peer-reviewed validation of APCA was found [convention]; the WCAG 3.0 Working Draft (10 September 2026) is not a requirement, so keep WCAG 2.2 ratios.
- **Color blindness**: red-green deficiency up to about 8% of males and 0.5% of females in populations of Northern European descent (Wikipedia); lower globally (Judd 1943, via Wikipedia) [verified]; one study sample gave 3.75% and 0.68% [verified]. Never encode meaning by hue alone [assumed standard practice].
- **Not covered**: dark-mode design, tonal palettes and semantic roles (Material 3 pages failed to load), cultural color meaning. Common dark-mode mistake is inverting a light palette, which keeps full chroma and causes glare [assumed].

## 6. Accessibility and inclusive design

- **POUR**: perceivable, operable, understandable, robust [verified]. **WCAG 2.2 adds 9 criteria and removes 4.1.1 Parsing.** AA: 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum), 2.5.7 Dragging Movements, 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum), 3.3.8 Accessible Authentication (Minimum). A: 3.2.6 Consistent Help, 3.3.7 Redundant Entry. AAA: 2.4.12, 2.4.13 Focus Appearance, 3.3.9 [verified].
- **Specifics.** Target size: 24 x 24 CSS px, or a 24 px circle that does not overlap another target; five exceptions [verified]. Dragging needs a single-pointer alternative; a keyboard alternative alone does not satisfy it [verified]. Accessible authentication: no memorising or transcribing unless there is an alternative; do not block paste or autofill [verified]. Focus Not Obscured needs only part of the focused element visible; fix sticky headers with scroll padding [verified]. Focus Appearance (AAA): 2 CSS px perimeter and 3:1 change [verified].
- **Conformance**: A, AA, AAA are cumulative; AAA is not advised as a blanket target; a claim must cover full pages and processes [verified]. Default target AA [estimated].
- **ARIA**: first rule is to use native HTML if it has the semantics; the source document is a discontinued draft, so use the ARIA Authoring Practices Guide as the live reference [verified]. WebAIM Million 2026: 82.7% of home pages used ARIA and averaged 59.1 errors vs 42 without [verified]. Correlation, not proof of cause.
- **WebAIM Million 2026** (eighth edition, February 2026 data, published 30 March 2026): 95.9% of home pages had detectable WCAG 2 failures, 56.1 errors per page; low contrast 83.9%, missing alt 53.1%, missing form labels 51.0%, empty links 46.3%, empty buttons 30.6%, missing language 13.5% [verified].
- **Screen readers** (WebAIM survey #11, July to August 2026, n=1,780): primary desktop reader JAWS 55.0%, NVDA 32.9%, VoiceOver 6.5%; 42.0% commonly use VoiceOver on desktop [verified]. Survey #10 (Dec 2023 to Jan 2024, n=1,539) had JAWS 40.5%, NVDA 37.7%, VoiceOver 43.9% commonly used.
- **Motion**: honor `prefers-reduced-motion` (baseline since January 2020); replace large scaling or panning with gentler motion such as fades [verified]. WCAG 2.3.3: interaction-triggered motion must be disable-able; color or opacity changes alone do not count [verified].
- **Inclusive design** (Microsoft): recognise exclusion, learn from diversity, solve for one and extend to many; the persona spectrum is permanent, temporary, situational [verified].
- **Law (secondary sources only)**. EU European Accessibility Act applied from 28 June 2025 for new in-scope products and services; some pre-existing service contracts run to 2030 [estimated detail]; micro-enterprises may be exempt [verified, law-firm summary]. **US ADA Title II** (state and local governments): DOJ interim final rule of 20 April 2026 moved the deadline for populations of 50,000 or more from 24 April 2026 to 26 April 2027, and smaller entities and special districts to 26 April 2028; the standard stays WCAG 2.1 AA; comments ran to 22 June 2026 [rechecked]. Title III (private sector) status: unknown. Federal Register 2026-07663 gives 26 April 2027 (50,000 or more) and 26 April 2028 (smaller entities and special districts).
- **Testing**: automated scan, keyboard-only pass, screen reader pass (NVDA or VoiceOver), 200% zoom, reduced-motion, contrast check [assumed]. The claim that automation catches 30 to 40% of issues is [assumed].
- **Not covered**: cognitive-disability prevalence and best practice beyond the three WCAG hooks (3.3.7, 3.3.8, 3.2.6).

## 7. Interaction, states, feedback, motion

- **Response-time limits** (NN/g): (limits trace to Miller 1968, https://doi.org/10.1145/1476589.1476628, and Card et al. 1991, https://doi.org/10.1145/108844.108874, via Nielsen 1993; these are [industry] popularisations, not IBM-report claims) 0.1 s feels instant; 1 s keeps train of thought, show work is happening beyond it; 10 s is the attention limit, show percent-done and a way to cancel [verified]. INP: good at or under 200 ms, poor above 500 ms, at the 75th percentile [verified].
- **Loading**: under 1 s show neither skeleton nor spinner; about 2 to 10 s spinner for a single module or skeleton for a full page; over 10 s a progress bar with an estimate; skeletons only for full-page loads, not uploads or conversions; header-only skeletons act like a spinner [verified]. No quantitative perceived-speed benefit for skeletons was found [verified].
- **Motion** (legacy Material, the current M3 page failed to load): mobile about 300 ms; large full-screen about 375 ms; over 400 ms feels slow; desktop 150 to 200 ms; entering about 225 ms, leaving about 195 ms; decelerate on enter, accelerate on exit [verified]. A broad 100 to 500 ms band is [assumed]; the sourced range is about 150 to 400 ms. Apple: reduce automatic and repetitive motion, replace x/y/z transitions with fades, avoid animating into blur [verified].
- **Touch targets** (state unit and level each time; CSS px, pt and dp are not interchangeable): WCAG 2.5.8 (AA) 24x24 CSS px, 2.5.5 (AAA) 44x44 CSS px; Apple 44 x 44 pt default, 28 x 28 pt minimum on iOS, about 12 pt padding around bezeled and 24 pt around borderless elements; Android 48 x 48 dp with at least 8 dp between [verified]. Apple visionOS default 60 pt (minimum 28 pt); macOS 28 default, 20 minimum [verified]. Summary: WCAG 24 CSS px AA / 44 CSS px AAA; Apple 44 pt default, 28 pt min; Android 48 dp plus 8 dp.
- **Touch-target research** [experiment]: Parhi, Karlson and Bederson (2006, *MobileHCI '06*, https://doi.org/10.1145/1152215.1152260) found targets of at least 9.2 mm (single) and 9.6 mm (multiple) worked well; Bergstrom-Lehtovirta and Oulasvirta (2014, *CHI '14*, https://doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557354) give a thumb-reach model. - **Forms**: Baymard: check most fields on blur, fixed-length fields (ZIP, card, CVV) once complete, clear errors on each keystroke once valid, show a positive cue; errors only on submit made people stop, hunt and retype [verified]. - **Form validation timing** [experiment]: Bargas-Avila et al. - **Microinteractions** (Saffer): trigger, rules, feedback, loops and modes; add loops only if they add value [verified, via secondary summary].
- **State set** for each component: default, hover, focus, active, disabled, loading, empty, error, success [estimated]. ## 8. Information architecture, navigation, writing

- **Hidden navigation costs.** NN/g, 179 participants, 6 sites: hidden desktop nav used in 27% of cases vs 48% visible; mobile 57% hidden vs 86% combined; content discoverability fell by more than 20%; desktop tasks at least 39% slower. Show links directly on mobile if there are 4 or fewer top-level items [verified].
- **Depth vs breadth**: avoid extremes; add intermediate pages instead of a 32-item flat list; for deep sites add breadcrumbs and shortcuts. NN/g gives no task-success data [verified].
- **Breadcrumbs**: show structural path, current page unlinked, usually unnecessary on 1 to 2 level sites; they do not replace navigation [verified].
- **Card sort vs tree test**: card sorting is generative and early; tree testing is evaluative and strips visual cues, so results may not match the finished site [verified]. Sample sizes in section 9.
- **Error messages** (GOV.UK): put the message beside the field and repeat in a summary; keep entered answers; avoid "sorry", "valid", "invalid", "please" and generic "An error occurred"; include a hidden "Error:" prefix for screen readers [verified].
- **Empty states**: never blank; distinguish loading, failed and empty; add a learning cue and a direct action [verified].
- **Localization**: strings up to 10 characters can grow 200 to 300%, over 70 characters about 130% (the source table's 51 to 70 row reads 151 to 170%, probably a typo); Chinese and Korean glyphs about twice the width; Thai needs about 150% vertical space (IBM-derived, W3C) [verified]. RTL mirroring rules: unknown.
- **Chat and AI interfaces** (NN/g): state capabilities in the first message, offer clickable suggested questions, do not autoscroll during streaming, use progressive disclosure [verified].
- **Not covered**: plain-language reading-grade targets, button-label research, bottom-nav counts (3 to 5 is common practice [assumed]), Rosenfeld and Morville components.

## 9. Research and validation

- **Method landscape** (NN/g): three axes, attitudinal vs behavioral, qualitative vs quantitative, and context of use; by stage, generative, formative, summative; lean on behavior over stated belief [verified].
- **Five users, honestly.** One user finds about 31% of problems; n users find N x (1-(1-L)^n); five find about 84% by the formula (NN/g rounds to 85%) [verified in the 1993 paper]; the paper's own cost-benefit optimum is about 4.4 evaluators, and the 5-user rule was framed for iterative rounds. Limits: the formula needs N, which testing is meant to find; distinct user groups need 3 to 4 each [verified]. A reanalysis of nine expert teams (Lindgaard and Chattratichart, CHI 2007) found no reliable link between users tested and problems found (teams found 7 to 43% of known problems) but a link with number of tasks [verified, via a secondary newsletter]. Implication: more tasks and groups beat a fixed n; run several small rounds [estimated].
- **Bias**: confirmation bias enters question design, data collection and analysis; test rough designs early, triangulate with analytics and support logs, get outside review [verified].
- **SUS**: odd items minus 1, even items 5 minus response, sum x 2.5; average 68 from about 500 studies (peer-reviewed anchor: Lewis and Sauro 2018, *J. Usability Studies* 13(3), 158-167, https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/3294033.3294037); report a confidence interval [verified]. Caveats: not a percentage (normalise to a percentile); r about .24 with task performance; items 4 and 10 form a learnability subscale. Caveats: not a percentage (normalise to a percentile); r about .24 with task performance; items 4 and 10 form a learnability subscale. **NPS**: cutoffs (9 to 10 promoters, 0 to 6 detractors) are conventional; Pollack and Alexandrov (2013) found no empirical evidence NPS predicts growth [verified].
- **Card sorting sample**: NN/g recommends 15; correlation with large-sample results about 0.75 at 5, 0.90 at 15, 0.95 at 30, 0.98 at 60; Tullis and Wood say 20 to 30 [verified]. Tree testing sample: unknown.
- **Choice overload**: Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010, *J. Consumer Research* 37(3), https://doi.org/10.1086/651235) [meta-analysis]: 63 conditions from 50 experiments, N = 5,036; mean effect "virtually zero" (D = 0.02, 95% CI -0.09 to 0.12) with large variance across studies. A counterweight to "fewer options" folklore; it does not show that more options always help either.
- **A/B testing**: peeking inflates false positives; fix N in advance. In Evan Miller's sequential test (alpha 0.05, power 80%) the maximum conversions to collect are 170 for a 50% lift and 2,922 for a 10% lift (successes, not visitors); fixed-sample sizes depend on baseline rate [verified]. 20 tests at alpha 0.05 give about a 64% chance of at least one false win (arithmetic 1-0.95^20; no source opened); Bonferroni is conservative, Benjamini-Hochberg controls false discovery rate [verified].
- **Personas**: fictional but must rest on research; each detail should serve the design; expert review with personas does not replace testing [verified].
- **Not covered**: jobs-to-be-done validity, synthesis methods, survey sampling, winner's curse, interview technique beyond bias, continuous discovery (Torres) beyond its name.

**Mobile vs tablet vs desktop: evidence** [verified unless noted]
- **Usage**: StatCounter, October 2026, worldwide page views: mobile 59.9%, desktop 38.51%, tablet 1.59% (over 3 billion monthly page views; the exact window behind the figure is unclear). Implication [estimated]: the main layout decision is phone vs wider screens; tablet-specific layouts are a targeted investment. Viewport-size distribution: unknown.
- **Grip and thumb reach: weaker than its reputation.** Hoober (2013, UXmatters) [industry observation, later updated and superseded by newer guidance, not peer-reviewed]: 1,333 observations of 780 people touching the screen; one-handed 49%, cradled 36%, two-handed 15%. He calls his thumb-zone charts approximate, later said the drawings were inaccurate and should be ignored, and cites later work showing people touch screen centres faster and more accurately than corners. The earlier 70% two-handed phablet figure is not on the cited UXmatters column (unsourced). The "thumbs drive 75% of interactions" figure is uncited.
- **Bottom vs top navigation**: no controlled comparison found. The one number is second-hand: Spotify's move from a top-left menu to a bottom bar raised clicks 9% overall and 30% on menu items (TechCrunch 2016, via Smashing). Treat bottom-nav superiority as [estimated], weak evidence.
- **Tablet guidance**: Apple split views suit regular width, iPadOS supports two or three vertical panes, design for narrow, compact and intermediate widths because windows resize fluidly, and let people hide and restore panes. Material canonical layouts: list-detail shows panes side by side on expanded width and one at a time on medium or compact; supporting pane is about 70/30 in landscape on expanded, an equal split at medium, and below or in a sheet at compact; feed goes from one column to a grid. - **Mobile checkout** (Baymard, 50 sites, 2015 snapshot): 33% did not show total cost before card entry; 28% asked for the same information twice; 92% showed a generic error message; 60% of test subjects missed guest checkout. No same-time desktop comparison; form-completion numbers desktop vs mobile: unknown.

**Viewport mechanics** [verified unless noted]
- **Meta viewport**: `width=device-width` makes the layout viewport equal device width (without it some mobile browsers use a wider virtual viewport, about 980 px cited). `initial-scale=1` is usually unnecessary. `user-scalable=no` and `maximum-scale` are ignored by iOS 10+ by default and block low-vision users; MDN notes WCAG expects at least 2x scaling. The W3C test rule "Meta viewport allows for zoom" ties the tag to 1.4.4; tying it to 1.4.10 is inference [estimated].
- **Reflow and resize**: 1.4.10 requires vertical content to work at 320 CSS px wide and horizontal content at 256 CSS px high (400% zoom), with narrow exceptions (maps, diagrams, video, games, data tables, persistent toolbars); 1.4.4 requires text to resize to 200% without loss. 1.3.4 allows fixed orientation only when essential (bank check, piano, projector slides, VR).
- **Viewport units**: on mobile `100vh` stays fixed while browser toolbars move, so content can overflow on load. `lvh` assumes the browser UI is retracted, `svh` that it is expanded, `dvh` tracks the current state. `dvh`/`svh`/`lvh` are Baseline Widely Available since 2025-06-05 (Chrome 108, Firefox 101, Safari 15.4). The on-screen keyboard does not change viewport units by default; scrollbars are not counted, so `100vw` can overflow.
- **Safe areas**: `env(safe-area-inset-*)` is Baseline Widely Available (MDN: since January 2020); pair `viewport-fit=cover` with safe-area variables. Whether cover is required for non-zero insets: unknown.
- **Keyboard and visual viewport**: pinch zoom and the keyboard shrink the visual viewport while the layout viewport stays the same; `visualViewport` is widely available (since August 2021). `interactive-widget` values are `resizes-visual` (default), `resizes-content`, `overlays-content`; Chrome 108+ only, not Chrome on iOS or iPadOS. Virtual Keyboard API details: unknown.
- **Default breakpoints** (min-width unless stated): Tailwind sm 640, md 768, lg 1024, xl 1280, 2xl 1536 px (rem-based); Bootstrap 5.3 576, 768, 992, 1200, 1400 px (max-width queries subtract 0.02 px because range queries were not yet available); Carbon 320, 672, 1056, 1312, 1584 px; Android window classes compact under 600 dp, medium under 840, expanded under 1200, large under 1600, extra-large 1600 and up, explicitly not device-based; GOV.UK gives no numeric breakpoints on its layout page (max width 1020 px, design for ranges, not devices). Material 3 web values: likely the same as Android [assumed].
- **em vs px**: MDN says em works better when users change text size; Bootstrap uses px; Tailwind uses rem and warns against mixing. How to choose content-based breakpoints: no fetched source describes a method [unknown].
- **Foldables**: Chrome and Edge 138 support `horizontal-viewport-segments` (Limited availability, no Firefox or Safari); documented variables are `env(viewport-segment-*)`, not `env(fold-*)`. Android guidance: tabletop and book postures, keep dialogs and menus off the fold.
- **Range syntax** (`width >= 600px`, `400px <= width <= 700px`) is documented on MDN; its Baseline status was not fetched, likely widely available [assumed].

### Platform docs audit: Apple HIG and Android (added 2026-11-12, two Haiku agents; Apple foldable page re-fetched by the main session)

**Foldables, both platforms**
- **Apple** has foldable guidance: "Designing for iPhone Duo" (change log dated September 9, 2026, listed as a new page) [rechecked]. **Apple HIG: additions** [verified]

## 12. Learning roadmap and drills [estimated, not sourced]

1. **0 to 6 months, foundations**: read Norman and Krug; run weekly heuristic teardowns of products you use against Nielsen's 10, scoring each issue 0 to 4.
2. **6 to 12 months, critique reps**: critique your own work weekly against a written rubric (goal, evidence, alternatives); record decisions and reasons.
3. **12 to 24 months, ship**: take one feature end to end with a defined HEART-style metric, a baseline and a post-launch readout.
4. **24+ months, strategy and ethics**: write a one-page strategy memo; audit a live cancel flow against Gray's five categories and the legal points in section 11.

**Drills**: redesign a daily flow after writing the problem statement; run a 5-second test on three screens and write what the primary action was; audit one form for label, error and validation timing against section 7; check a page at 200% zoom, keyboard only, and reduced motion; critique an AI chat product on uncertainty, sources and error recovery.

**Measuring progress** (proposed): rubric-scored critiques monthly (trend, not one score); share of shipped work with a metric and readout; decisions changed by your evidence, reviewed by a peer.
